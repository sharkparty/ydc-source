# Foreign Money and the Emoluments Clauses

Foreign states paying the president's family while the president sets policy
toward those states. This is the constitutional provision most people have never
heard of, and the one this record breaks most consistently.

← [Back to the index](README.md)

## What the Constitution actually says

> "No Person holding any Office of Profit or Trust under them, shall, without
> the Consent of the Congress, accept of any present, Emolument, Office, or
> Title, of any kind whatever, from any King, Prince, or foreign State."
>
> — Article I, Section 9, Clause 8

There is no de minimis exception, no "but it was a fair market transaction"
carve-out, and no enforcement mechanism other than Congress. That last part is
the whole problem.

Congressional Democrats have introduced resolutions on the foreign and domestic
Emoluments Clauses, accusing the president of

> "selling out U.S. foreign policy to Kings, Princes and foreign states,
> collecting millions and billions of dollars from these prohibited foreign
> sources, through clandestine crypto schemes, his hotels and golf courses and
> outrageous family business dealings."

([Rep. Raskin, House Judiciary Democrats](https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/ranking-member-raskin-introduces-dual-resolutions-demanding-trump-comply-with-constitution-s-foreign-and-domestic-emoluments-clauses-amid-a-rampage-of-presidential-profiteering-and-plunder),

## 1. Saudi Arabia: the arms deal and Kushner's $2 billion

Trump approved a **$142 billion arms package** with Saudi Arabia — F-35s, tanks,
the full catalogue — while his son-in-law and former senior adviser Jared
Kushner held a **$2 billion investment from Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin
Salman** in his private equity firm, Affinity Partners.

The Saudi fund's own advisory panel had recommended against the investment,
citing Affinity's inexperience. They were overruled.

Progressive Caucus leaders condemned the administration for "prioritizing
weapons-contractor profits and his own family's business interests."
Public-interest groups described it as a "complete merger" of Trump family
business interests with U.S. foreign relations.
([The New Republic](https://newrepublic.com/post/195180/trump-arms-deal-saudi-arabia-mbs#1))

**Context this belongs next to:** in November 2018, after the CIA concluded that
the Crown Prince had ordered the murder and dismemberment of U.S.-based
journalist Jamal Khashoggi, Trump publicly defended him and bypassed Congress to
push Saudi arms sales through. See [Timeline](timeline.md).

## 3. Qatar: the $400 million airplane

Qatar gave the United States a luxury Boeing 747 valued at roughly **$400
million** for Trump's use as Air Force One. Qatari officials initially denied it
was a gift. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth accepted it after Trump's May 2025
visit to Qatar.

Trump said the aircraft would go to his **presidential library** after he leaves
office — which is the detail that converts a state-to-state transfer into a
personal one.

## 7. Turning off the anti-bribery law

In the second term the United States **suspended enforcement of the Foreign
Corrupt Practices Act.**

Since 1977 the FCPA had made the U.S. the world's leading enforcer against
corporate bribery of foreign officials — the single most consequential
anti-corruption statute any country has. Suspending it does not merely stop
American prosecutions; it removes the deterrent that shaped global corporate
compliance.

Analysts noted the U.S. approach was converging on models seen in Russia and
China, where "business relationships often take priority over governance
issues."
([World Journal](https://www.worldjournal.com/wj/story/121148/9666881?from=wj_top9topic_index))

Of everything in this file, this is the item with the longest half-life. The
payments are individually reversible. A dismantled enforcement norm is not.

## 9. Demanding a pardon for a foreign prime minister

In February 2026 Trump publicly pressured Israeli President Isaac Herzog to
pardon Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu in his corruption trial, calling Herzog

applied to an ally's justice system: a head of state under indictment should be
excused, and a head of state who declines to excuse him is the one behaving
