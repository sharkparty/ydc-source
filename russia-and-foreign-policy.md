## 1. Manafort: from Putin's man in Kyiv to Trump's campaign chairman

Paul Manafort and Rick Gates ran the Ukrainian campaign of Viktor Yanukovych —
the pro-Kremlin president whose ouster in 2014 Putin used as the pretext for
seizing Crimea.
([TIME](https://time.com/5003623/paul-manafort-mueller-indictment-ukraine-russia/))

Manafort then became chairman of Trump's 2016 presidential campaign, with Gates
as his deputy.

Manafort subsequently pleaded guilty to **conspiracy against the United
States** — a charge arising from his undisclosed work on behalf of a foreign
government.
([Vox, on what the charge means](https://www.vox.com/2018/9/14/17860410/conspiracy-against-the-united-states-paul-manafort-plea))

Trump pardoned him on December 23, 2020, sparing him the bulk of a 7½-year
sentence. The same clemency batch covered Roger Stone and Charles Kushner.
([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2020/12/23/949820820/trump-pardons-roger-stone-paul-manafort-and-charles-kushner),
[The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2020/12/24/trumps-pardon-paul-manafort-brings-full-circle-undermining-russia-investigation/))

The short version: a man who helped install Putin's preferred president in
Ukraine ran the campaign that installed Trump in the United States, was
convicted of conspiring against the United States on behalf of a foreign
government, and was pardoned by the man he had elected.

## 2. The 2016 campaign's own conduct

Three episodes that are not allegations — they are on the record, in writing or
on camera:

**June 2016 — the Trump Tower meeting.** Donald Trump Jr., Jared Kushner, and
campaign chairman Paul Manafort met a Kremlin-connected lawyer after being
offered damaging material on Hillary Clinton described in the email invitation as
coming "from Russia and its government." Trump Jr.'s reply, which he later
released himself: *"if it's what you say I love it."*

**July 2016 — "Russia, if you're listening."** At a televised press conference,
Trump publicly asked a hostile foreign intelligence service to find his
opponent's missing emails. Russian operatives attempted to target Clinton's
office for the first time that same day, per the Mueller indictment of GRU
officers.

**Throughout — the Moscow tower.** He was pursuing a Trump Tower Moscow deal for
most of the campaign while publicly denying any business with Russia. See
[Business Record and Fraud](business-and-fraud.md#9-trump-tower-moscow).

**What this does and does not establish:** the Mueller investigation did *not*
establish a criminal conspiracy — see
[Legal Record](legal-record.md#the-russia-investigation). No such finding is
required for the conduct above, which is undisputed by the participants, who
published the underlying material themselves.

## 3. Helsinki, and taking Putin's word over his own agencies

**July 2018.** Standing beside Vladimir Putin at a joint press conference in
Finland, Trump publicly sided with the Russian president against the unanimous
assessment of U.S. intelligence agencies on Russian interference in 2016. He
walked it back the next day by claiming he had misspoken a single word.

**May 2017.** In an Oval Office meeting with the Russian Foreign Minister and
Ambassador — from which American press were excluded, while Russian state
photographers were admitted — Trump disclosed code-word classified intelligence
reportedly provided by Israel, jeopardizing the source and the sharing
arrangement behind it.

## 4. Crimea and sanctions relief

After Putin seized Crimea in 2014, the Obama administration imposed sanctions on
Russia. Trump later told G7 leaders that Crimea is Russian — effectively
endorsing the legitimacy of the annexation — and pressed for Russia's
readmission to the group.
([BuzzFeed News, "Trump Told G7 Leaders That Crimea Is Russian"](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/albertonardelli/trump-russia-crimea))

**Correction to claims in circulation:** Trump did not repeal the Crimea
sanctions wholesale, and in 2017 he signed the CAATSA sanctions bill, under a
veto-proof majority he had opposed. The documented action is narrower: in January
2019 the Treasury Department lifted sanctions on
Rusal, EN+ Group, and EuroSibEnergo, the companies controlled by Putin-aligned
oligarch Oleg Deripaska. A Senate resolution to block the move failed 57–42,
short of the 60 votes needed, even though eleven Republicans crossed over to
oppose the administration. Deripaska personally remained sanctioned; his
companies did not.
([CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2019/01/27/politics/trump-admin-lifts-sanctions-oleg-deripaska/index.html),
[RFE/RL](https://www.rferl.org/a/us-sanctions-lifted-rusal-deripaska-russia-kremlin/29734352.html),
[The Moscow Times](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2019/01/16/senate-puts-forward-a-resolution-to-keep-sanctions-on-deripaska-companies-a64152),
[Atlantic Council analysis of the deal terms](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/econographics/us-sanctions-lifted-coles-2/))

## 5. Syria: handing over the bases, abandoning the Kurds

On October 13, 2019, Trump ordered a withdrawal of U.S. forces from northern
Syria.
([The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/10/13/us/politics/mark-esper-syria-kurds-turkey.html))

Russian forces moved into the abandoned American air base within weeks.
([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-syria-security-russia/russia-lands-forces-at-former-u-s-air-base-in-northern-syria-idUSKBN1XP0XN))

The consequences for the Kurdish forces who had done the ground fighting against
ISIS on America's behalf:

- Turkey's offensive killed hundreds and displaced thousands.
  ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-syria-security-turkey-usa/thousands-flee-hundreds-reported-dead-in-turkish-attack-on-us-allied-kurds-in-syria-idUSKBN1WP0VH))
- More than a hundred ISIS prisoners escaped custody in the chaos.
  ([ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/US/100-isis-prisoners-loose-security-officials-act-now/story?id=66499973))
- The cost to America's credibility as a partner outlasted the withdrawal
  itself. ([Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2019/10/18/trump-can-forget-burden-sharing-now/))

For the argument about *when* this order was given relative to the impeachment
inquiry, see [Timelines and Open Questions](timelines-and-open-questions.md).

## 6. Georgia: the borderization nobody answered

Russia has been advancing its de facto border with Georgia by physically moving
fences and boundary markers at night, annexing land a few hundred meters at a
time — a practice known as "borderization" or creeping occupation. It continued
throughout Trump's term. The administration did not respond.
([LSE Human Rights blog](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/humanrights/2019/02/05/russias-new-strategy-in-georgia-creeping-occupation/))

## 7. Belarus: the test case, failed

Alexander Lukashenko, Putin's client dictator in Belarus, claimed victory in an
obviously rigged 2020 election, triggering mass protests.

The structural parallel is exact: Ukraine's 2013–14 protests against Putin's
client Yanukovych received American backing, Yanukovych fled, and Ukraine
elected a government that was not Moscow's. When the same opening appeared in
Belarus with Trump in office, the United States did essentially nothing.
([Foreign Policy, "The Trump Administration Has Gone AWOL on Belarus"](https://foreignpolicy.com/2020/09/03/belarus-election-fraud-trump-lukashenko-putin/))

## 10. The Soleimani strike

On January 3, 2020, at Trump's direction, the U.S. military killed Qassem
Soleimani — commander of Iran's Quds Force and a serving official of a state the
U.S. was not at war with — in an air strike near Baghdad International Airport.
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2020/01/trump-assassination-soleimani-200103172526102.html),
[CNBC, on who Soleimani was and why the killing mattered](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/01/03/who-was-iranian-general-qasem-soleimani-and-why-his-killing-matters.html))

The administration justified the strike as a response to an *imminent* threat,
then repeatedly failed to substantiate that claim; its formal report to Congress
dropped the imminence argument.
([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/02/14/trump-administration-issues-report-on-soleimani-killing.html))

**Context:** Soleimani was a legitimate object of loathing — he directed
operations that killed Americans. The objection here is not that he was a
sympathetic figure. It is that an act of war against a state actor was ordered
on a justification the administration could not defend a month later, and that
the timing deserves scrutiny. See
[Timelines and Open Questions](timelines-and-open-questions.md).

The Defense Department statement, Trump's own remarks, and the Office of Legal
Counsel memo that justified the strike are in
[War Powers and Military Strikes](war-powers-and-military-strikes.md), alongside
the 2025–2026 campaigns built on the same legal theory.

## The pattern

Each item above has an available defense in isolation. In aggregate they
constitute a consistent record: a campaign chairman who had worked for Putin's
preferred president in Kyiv, sanctions relief for a Putin-aligned oligarch over
bipartisan objection, a public endorsement of the annexation of Crimea, an
abandoned Syrian base occupied by Russian forces within weeks, no response to
Georgia, no response to Belarus, a concealed Moscow tower deal pursued during the
campaign, and public praise for the invasion of Ukraine.

No element of this required Russian fabrication. It required only that the
sequence continue and that each item be assessed separately from the others.
